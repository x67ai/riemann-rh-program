## §0 The question and the answer

**Question (the brief, verbatim in substance).** k = F_q, S a smooth geometrically connected curve over k, E its set of closed points. Borger's Λ_S-formalism defines a second square, S ×_{F₁^S} S := W_S*(S), the E-typical Witt space of S over itself (the Λ_S-analog of 0906.3146 §1.2's definition for Spec Z). Weil's proof of RH for S on the surface S ×_k S has a zero-dimensional half (Z) — (Γ_{Fr^N} · Δ) = N_N = #S(F_{q^N}) — and a quadratic-form half (Q) — (Δ·Δ) = 2 − 2g, (Γ_Fr·Γ_Fr) = q(2 − 2g), the fiber numbers, and the Castelnuovo–Severi inequality (D·D) ≤ 2d₁d₂ from the Hodge index theorem. Which of (Z), (Q) is expressed on W_S*(S) in the host's own terms (components, scheme-theoretic intersections, arithmetic degrees), and which lives only on S ×_k S?

**Answer, in the 10(c) shape — NO, as expected, with the sharper form proved.** *The quadratic-form half of Weil's proof cannot be expressed on the host W_S*(S) because the host is one-dimensional (Borger 1006.0092 Prop. 16.5(d)): its irreducible components Γ_n (one for each effective divisor n on S, §1.5) are not Cartier divisors, so no component has a self-intersection number, no line bundle O(Γ_n), no normal bundle, and no intersection form on which a Hodge-index inequality could be stated; its intrinsic pairing is defined between DISTINCT components only, and equals* deg(Γ_m ∩ Γ_n) = (1 + min(m_𝔪, n_𝔪))·deg(𝔪)·log q *when m and n differ at exactly one closed point 𝔪, and 0 when they differ at two or more (Theorem 3.3); these numbers are equivalent information to the zeta function Z(S, t) and to nothing beyond it (Theorem 4.2), so any inequality imposed on them is an inequality on Z(S, t)'s own coefficients — III.20 (B)'s "the generator never acts on the zeta's own explicit formula", here a rung-1 theorem — and the only expression of (Q) on the host is its conclusion. The zero-dimensional half IS expressed:* Σ_{|n| = N} deg(Γ_0 ∩ Γ_n) = log q · N_N = log q · (Γ_{Fr^N} · Δ) *for every N ≥ 1 (Theorem 3.4; checked on the g = 1 model y² = x³ + x + 1 over F₇ at N = 1, 2, 3, 4 against 5, 55, 380, 2475), and its generating series is* log q · t·Z′(S, t)/Z(S, t). *The passage from S ×_k S to the host is the comparison map (7.3.2) read at the page: the ghost component n maps onto the graph of Fr_q^{|n|}, |n| = Σ_𝔪 n_𝔪 deg 𝔪, and the index 0 onto the diagonal; it is not injective on components (two closed points of the same degree give distinct components with one image, Borger p. 25), so the host carries a finer index set than the surface — effective divisors, not integers — and it is exactly the single-point divisors that carry the nonzero pairings.* So: 1 of 2 halves expressed. ## §1 The definitions, fixed from the printed pages (each with its 10(n) line)

**1.1 The curve and its points.** k = F_q; S a smooth, geometrically connected curve over k, complete unless said otherwise; E the set of closed points of S; for 𝔪 ∈ E, deg 𝔪 := [O_S/𝔪 : k] and q_𝔪 := #(O_S/𝔪) = q^{deg 𝔪}; a_d := #{𝔪 ∈ E : deg 𝔪 = d}; N_N := #S(F_{q^N}) = Σ_{d | N} d·a_d. N^{(E)} := ⊕_E N (Borger 0801.1691 p. 2), the monoid of effective divisors on S; for n ∈ N^{(E)}, |n| := deg(n) := Σ_𝔪 n_𝔪 deg 𝔪 (this is Borger's deg(n), 0906.3146 p. 25, §7.3); e_𝔪 the basis vector; supp(n) := {𝔪 : n_𝔪 > 0}. For an affine open U = Spec R ⊆ S, R is a Dedekind domain and each 𝔪 ∈ E ∩ U is a maximal ideal of R with finite residue field: it is *supramaximal* in Borger's sense (0801.1691 §1.1 p. 6: "(a) R/𝔪 is a finite field, R_𝔪 is a discrete valuation ring, and 𝔪 is finitely presented as an R-module" — true here because S is smooth of dimension 1, so O_{S,𝔪} is a regular local ring of dimension 1, and R is noetherian; p. 6: "By far the most important example is a maximal ideal with finite residue field in a Dedekind domain"), and distinct maximal ideals are pairwise coprime as §1.2 p. 7 requires. *10(n):* the objects are Borger's; nothing new.

**1.2 The Λ_S-structure and the absolute point of F₁^S.** Read at the page: 0906.3146 §7.1 p. 24 — "Let S be a scheme of finite type over Z, and let E be a set of regular closed points of codimension 1. For each point s ∈ E, let q_s denote the cardinality of s. Let X be a flat algebraic space over S. We can then define a Λ_{S,E}-action on X just as we define Λ-actions, but now we use commuting endomorphisms ψ_s: X → X, one for each point s ∈ E, such that ψ_s agrees with the q_s-power Frobenius operator on the fiber X_s"; for E-flat rings, 0801.1691 §1.6 p. 8 (a Ψ_{R,E}-action such that id ⊗ ψ_α on R/𝔪_α ⊗_R A is x ↦ x^{q_α}) and §1.17 p. 12 ("a Λ_{R,E}-ring structure on an E-flat R-algebra A is the same as a commuting family of Frobenius lifts ψ_α"). **The identity family is a Λ_S-structure on S itself:** the fiber of S over s ∈ E is Spec(O_S/𝔪_s) = Spec F_{q_s}, on which the q_s-power map is the identity. Under it O_S with ψ = id is a Λ_{R,E}-ring on every affine open, and by 0801.1691 §1.17 p. 12 that structure is the coalgebra map R → W_{R,E}(R) given by the constant vectors, r ↦ ⟨r, r, r, …⟩ (see 1.3). *10(n):* 0906.3146 §2.1 p. 8 prints this for Z ("The ring Z has a unique Λ-structure — each ψ_p is the identity. Under this structure, it is the initial object in the category of Λ-rings. It is therefore reasonable to denote it F₁ and call Spec Z, viewed as a Λ-space, the absolute point"), and p. 8 §1.2 end says "everything above extends to the case where S is the spectrum of the ring of integers of any number field or any smooth curve over a finite field"; the sentence "S with the identity family is the absolute point of F₁^S-geometry" is the writer's one-line transport, not printed. (Uniqueness of the Λ_S-structure on O_S, the analog of "unique" in §2.1, is not needed and not claimed.)

**1.3 The E-typical Witt vectors of S over itself, W_S(R) := W_{R,E}(R).** Read at the page, 0801.1691 p. 2: "Let N^{(E)} denote the commutative monoid ⊕_E N … Given an action of N^{(E)} on an R-algebra B, let ψ_𝔪 denote the endomorphism of B given by the 𝔪-th element of the standard basis of N^{(E)}. Let us say that such an action is a Λ_{R,E}-structure if for each 𝔪 ∈ E, the map ψ_𝔪 reduces to the Frobenius endomorphism x ↦ x^{[R:𝔪]} on B/𝔪B. Now, for any R-algebra A, the monoid N^{(E)} acts on A^{N^{(E)}} through its translation action on itself in the exponent. When A is E-flat, we define W^fl_{R,E}(A) to be the largest of the sub-R-algebras B ⊆ A^{N^{(E)}} having the properties that B is stable under the action of N^{(E)} and that the induced action on B is a Λ_{R,E}-structure." §1.8 p. 9 constructs it: U₀(A) = A^{N^{(E)}}, U_{i+1}(A) = {b ∈ U_i(A) | ψ_α(b) − b^{q_α} ∈ 𝔪_α U_i(A) for all α ∈ E}, W^fl_{R,E}(A) = ∩_i U_i(A) (1.8.1). §1.12 p. 11 defines W_{R,E} on all R-algebras by (1.12.1), and "Its restriction to Ring^fl_R agrees with W^fl_{R,E}"; §1.14 p. 12: "When A is E-flat, it is harmless to identify [the ghost map] w with the inclusion map." R is a domain, hence E-flat (§1.3 p. 7), so W_S(R) = W_{R,E}(R) = W^fl_{R,E}(R) ⊆ R^{N^{(E)}}, and its n-th ghost component is the coordinate projection w_n: a ↦ a_n. (The name W_S is Borger's, 0906.3146 §7.3 p. 25: "W_S := W_{S,E} is the E-typical Witt vector functor over S".) **1.5 The ghost components Γ_n.** For n ∈ N^{(E)}, Γ_n: S → W_S*(S) is the map dual to the n-th ghost projection w_n: W_S(R) → R on each affine piece (0906.3146 p. 25, §7.3: the composition ∐_{N^{(E)}} T → W_S(T) → S ×_k T "with the ghost map γ"). w_n is surjective (the constant vectors ⟨r, r, r, …⟩ lie in W_S(R): Theorem 3.1, or directly from p. 2 since constants are shift-invariant and r^{q_𝔪} ≡ r mod 𝔪), so Γ_n is a closed immersion at every finite level, an isomorphism of S onto an irreducible component of the host (1006.0092 p. 5: "W₁*(X) is the coequalizer … of the two maps X₀ ⇉ X ∐ X"; "the space W_n*(X) can be constructed by gluing n+1 copies of X together … along their fibers modulo p, …, pⁿ"; Thm. 17.3 p. 39 in the single-ideal case). **1.6 The comparison map (7.3.2) with T = S, and what it does to each component.** Read at the page, 0906.3146 p. 25 §7.3: "let T be an affine space over S, the unit of the adjunction f_! ⊣ f* at v_!(T) is (7.3.1) v_!(T) → f*f_!v_!(T) = f*s_!(T) which, by definition, is a map (7.3.2) W_S(T) → S ×_k T … The composition ∐_{N^{(E)}} T → W_S(T) → S ×_k T with the ghost map γ is the map that, on the component n ∈ N^{(E)}, is simply (pr, Fr^n_T): T → S ×_k T where pr denotes the structure map T → S, and Fr^n_T is the Frobenius map defined on functions by x ↦ x^{q^{deg(n)}}, where deg(n) denotes Σ_𝔪 n_𝔪[O_S/𝔪 : k], the degree of the effective divisor corresponding to n. In particular, if T = S, then the image of this map is the union in S ×_k S of the graphs of all powers of the Frobenius map on S. These components are not disjoint. For instance, let x and y be two distinct closed points of S with the same residue field. Then the components of W_S(S) of indices x and y are distinct but have the same image in S ×_k S. ## §3 The zero-dimensional half (Z): H1–H3, with proofs

Throughout, U = Spec R is an affine open of S, E is the set of maximal ideals of R corresponding to closed points of U (the closed points of S outside U enter as unit ideals and impose nothing, 1.4), and L := {a ∈ R^{N^{(E)}} : a_{n+e_𝔪} − a_n ∈ 𝔪^{1+n_𝔪} for all 𝔪 ∈ E, n ∈ N^{(E)}}. For 𝔪 ∈ E, q_𝔪 = #(R/𝔪) is a power of p = char k, so p ∈ 𝔪 and q_𝔪 ∈ 𝔪. Two facts about a Dedekind domain are used: (D1) for coprime ideals I, J, I ∩ J = IJ; (D2) every nonzero ideal 𝔪 is invertible, 𝔪·𝔪^{-1} = R with 𝔪^{-1} = {x ∈ Frac R : x𝔪 ⊆ R} (Borger uses the same fact, 0801.1691 p. 6 "a supramaximal ideal 𝔪 is invertible as an R-module").

**Theorem 3.1 (H1, CONFIRMED — proved, not read).** W_S(R) = W_{R,E}(R) = L. That is, the E-typical Witt vectors of the curve over itself are the ghost vectors ⟨a_n⟩_{n ∈ N^{(E)}} with a_{n+e_𝔪} ≡ a_n (mod 𝔪^{n_𝔪+1}) for every closed point 𝔪 and every n — no Frobenius twist, because the Λ_S-structure of R is the identity (1.2).

*Proof.* By 1.3, W_{R,E}(R) = W^fl_{R,E}(R) is the largest sub-R-algebra B ⊆ R^{N^{(E)}} stable under the shifts ψ_𝔪 ((ψ_𝔪 a)_n = a_{n+e_𝔪}, 0801.1691 §1.7 p. 8) on which the shifts form a Λ_{R,E}-structure, i.e., ψ_𝔪(b) − b^{q_𝔪} ∈ 𝔪B for all b ∈ B, 𝔪 ∈ E (p. 2); and it equals ∩_i U_i(R) with the U_i of §1.8 (p. 9).

(⊇) *L is such a B.* L is an R-submodule (the conditions are R-linear), contains 1, and is closed under products: a_{n+e_𝔪}b_{n+e_𝔪} − a_n b_n = a_{n+e_𝔪}(b_{n+e_𝔪} − b_n) + b_n(a_{n+e_𝔪} − a_n) ∈ 𝔪^{1+n_𝔪}. It is shift-stable: (ψ_{𝔪′}a)_{n+e_𝔪} − (ψ_{𝔪′}a)_n = a_{n+e_{𝔪′}+e_𝔪} − a_{n+e_{𝔪′}} ∈ 𝔪^{1+(n+e_{𝔪′})_𝔪} ⊆ 𝔪^{1+n_𝔪}. Λ-structure: fix 𝔪, a ∈ L, q = q_𝔪, and put c := ψ_𝔪(a) − a^q, c_n = a_{n+e_𝔪} − a_n^q. (i) c_n ∈ 𝔪: a_n^q ≡ a_n (mod 𝔪) since R/𝔪 = F_q, and a_{n+e_𝔪} ≡ a_n (mod 𝔪). (ii) For 𝔪′ ≠ 𝔪: c_{n+e_{𝔪′}} − c_n = (a_{n+e_{𝔪′}+e_𝔪} − a_{n+e_𝔪}) − (a_{n+e_{𝔪′}}^q − a_n^q); the first bracket lies in 𝔪′^{1+n_{𝔪′}}; writing a_{n+e_{𝔪′}} = a_n + y with y ∈ 𝔪′^{1+n_{𝔪′}}, the second bracket (a_n + y)^q − a_n^q is a multiple of y; so c_{n+e_{𝔪′}} − c_n ∈ 𝔪′^{1+n_{𝔪′}}. ## §4 The quadratic-form half (Q): H4 decided — the NO theorem, in full

Fix a finite box F ⊂ N^{(E)} (1.4, Lemma 3.2) and write B := W_{R,E,F}(R) = the image of W_S(R) in R^F (0801.1691 p. 3: "W^fl_{R,E,n}(A) denote the image of the subring W^fl_{R,E}(A) ⊆ A^{N^{(E)}} under the canonical projection A^{N^{(E)}} → A^{[0,n]}"); by Lemma 3.2, B = L_F. Three facts: (F1) J·R^F ⊆ B (a vector all of whose entries lie in J satisfies every congruence).
