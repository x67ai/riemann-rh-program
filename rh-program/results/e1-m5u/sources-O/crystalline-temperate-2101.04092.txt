   One can introduce a more general notion of a crystalline temperate distribution, which
is by definition a temperate distribution α whose support Λ is a locally finite set and
such that the spectrum S (the support of the Fourier transform α b) is also a locally finite
set. See [Pal17], [Fav18], [LR20] where distributions of this type were studied.
   Examples of crystalline temperate distributions which are not crystalline measures
may be constructed by starting from a crystalline measure µ and applying, in addition
to the operations mentioned above, also a finite number of differentiations and multi-
plication by polynomials. The main result of the present paper is that any temperate
distribution on R whose support and spectrum are uniformly discrete sets, can be
obtained from Poisson’s summation formula by these basic operations:
Theorem 1.1. Let α be a temperate distribution on R such that Λ = supp(α) and
S = supp(b
         α) are uniformly discrete sets. Then α can be represented in the form
                             X                  X            (p)
                       α=         c(τ, ω, l, p)   λl e2πiλω δλ+τ               (1.3)
                               (τ,ω,l,p)             λ∈L

where L is a lattice, (τ, ω, l, p) goes through a finite set of quadruples such that τ, ω are
real numbers and l, p are nonnegative integers, and c(τ, ω, l, p) are complex numbers.

  Conversely, if α is a distribution of the form (1.3) then its support Λ and spectrum
S are uniformly discrete sets. In fact, Λ is contained in a finite union of translates of
the lattice L, while S is contained in a finite union of translates of the dual lattice L∗ .
   The proof of Theorem 1.1 is based on the approach developed in [LO13], [LO15] which
is generalized from the context of measures to temperate distributions.

                     4. Arithmetic structure of the support

    In this section our goal is to prove the following result:
Theorem 4.1. Let α be a temperate distribution on R such that Λ = supp(α) and
S = supp(b α) are both uniformly discrete sets. Then Λ is contained in a finite union of
translates of some lattice.
   The result was proved in [LO13], [LO15] in the case where α and α b are measures, and
it is generalized here to the context of temperate distributions. Theorem 4.1 constitutes
the first step in the proof of Theorem 1.1, where the next step consists of showing that
the distribution α must be of the form (1.3).

4.1. Recall that a set Λ ⊂ R is said to be uniformly discrete if there is δ > 0 such that
|λ′ − λ| ⩾ δ for any two distinct points λ, λ′ in Λ. The maximal constant δ with this
property is called the separation constant of Λ, and will be denoted by δ(Λ).
  One can check that if Λ is a uniformly discrete set then D(Λ) ⩽ 1/δ(Λ). In particular,
the density D(Λ) is finite.
   We say that a set Λ ⊂ R is relatively dense if there is a > 0 such that any interval
[x, x + a] contains at least one point from Λ.
    If Λ is uniformly discrete and also relatively dense, then Λ is called a Delone set.

                5. Poisson type structure of the distribution

  In this section we complete the proof of Theorem 1.1 by establishing the following:
Theorem 5.1. Let α be a temperate distribution on R. Suppose that Λ = supp(α) is
contained in a finite union of translates of a lattice L, and that S = supp(b
                                                                            α) is a locally
finite set. Then α can be represented in the form (1.3).

  Theorem 1.1 follows as a consequence of Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 5.1.
   The representation (1.3) implies that the distribution α can be obtained from the
Poisson summation formula by a finite number of basic operations: shifts, modulations,
differentiations, multiplication by polynomials, and taking linear combinations.

5.1. We will use the following two lemmas.
Lemma 5.2. Let z1 , . . . , zN be distinct nonzero complex numbers, let k1 , . . . , kN be
positive integers, and let K = k1 + · · · + kN . For each integer m, 0 ⩽ m ⩽ K − 1, and
for each pair of integers (j, l) satisfying 1 ⩽ j ⩽ N, 0 ⩽ l ⩽ kj − 1, we denote
                                  Mm,j,l := (2πim)l zjm .                             (5.1)
Let M be a K × K matrix whose rows are indexed by m and columns indexed by pairs
(j, l), such that the entry at row m and column (j, l) is Mm,j,l . Then M is invertible.

   This is due to the fact that the matrix M can be obtained by a finite number of
elementary column operations from a confluent Vandermonde matrix (sometimes also
referred to as a generalized Vandermonde matrix) which is known to be invertible, see,
for instance, [Kal84]. This type of matrix arises e.g. in the linear interpolation problem
asking to find a polynomial p(z) of degree not greater than K − 1 such that at each
node zj the values p(zj ), p′ (zj ), . . . , p(kj −1) (zj ) are prescribed.

                                        6. Remarks

6.1. We say that a set Λ ⊂ R has bounded density if it satisfies the condition
                               sup #(Λ ∩ [x, x + 1)) < +∞.                             (6.1)
                               x∈R

This holds if and only if Λ is the union of a finite number of uniformly discrete sets.
  One can prove the following version of Theorem 1.1, where the support Λ is not
assumed to be uniformly discrete but only to have bounded density:
Theorem 6.1. Let α be a temperate distribution on R satisfying (2.4) and (2.5), such
that the support Λ = supp(α) has bounded density, while the spectrum S = supp(bα) is
uniformly discrete. Then the conclusion of Theorem 1.1 holds.

   This is an extension of [LO17, Theorem 2.2] where the result was proved in the case
where α and its Fourier transform αb are measures. The proof is based on the fact that
Theorem 4.2 remains valid under the weaker assumption that Λ is a relatively dense set
of bounded density (not assumed to be uniformly discrete), see [LO17, Lemma 6.3].

6.2. There is an interesting question as to whether the result in Theorem 1.1 holds in
several dimensions. The problem is open even for measures:
  Let Λ, S be two uniformly discrete sets in Rd , d > 1. Suppose that there is a measure µ,
supp(µ) = Λ, whose distributional Fourier transform µ   b is also a measure, supp(bµ) = S.
Does it follow that Λ can be covered by a finite union of translates of several lattices?
   It was proved in [LO15] that the answer is affirmative if µ is a positive measure, and
in this case the support Λ can in fact be covered by a finite union of translates of a
single lattice. However an example in [Fav16, Section 2] shows that for signed measures
µ the support need not be contained in a finite union of translates of a single lattice.
