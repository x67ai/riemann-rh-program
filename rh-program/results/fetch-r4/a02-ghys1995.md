## §3. The answer from the source

### 3.1 The setting, as the introduction states it (printed p. 387, PDF 2)

French, verbatim (p. 387, first paragraph of the Introduction):

> Les feuilles d'une variété feuilletée compacte sont en général non compactes et leur topologie peut être extrêmement complexe. La richesse des possibilités est illustrée par le théorème suivant de J. Cantwell et L. Conlon [2]. Sur une variété compacte quelconque de dimension 3, il est possible de construire un feuilletage de dimension 2 dont l'une des feuilles est difféomorphe à une surface non compacte donnée *a priori*. Cependant, nous nous proposons de montrer que, un feuilletage étant fixé, la topologie de la "plupart" de ses feuilles est soumise à de sérieuses contraintes. Lorsque les feuilles sont de dimension 2, ces contraintes sont si fortes qu'elles décrivent complètement les surfaces qui peuvent apparaître "génériquement". Avant de discuter ce que nous entendons par généricité, nous citons une application typique de nos résultats. Nous nous plaçons en fait dans le contexte des laminations, plus général que celui des feuilletages, où l'espace ambiant n'est pas supposé être une variété mais un espace métrique compact quelconque (voir §2 pour une définition).

English (my translation):

> The leaves of a compact foliated manifold are in general non-compact and their topology can be extremely complex. The richness of the possibilities is illustrated by the following theorem of J. Cantwell and L. Conlon [2]. On any compact manifold of dimension 3, it is possible to construct a foliation of dimension 2 one of whose leaves is diffeomorphic to a non-compact surface given *a priori*. However, we propose to show that, once a foliation is fixed, the topology of "most" of its leaves is subject to serious constraints. When the leaves are of dimension 2, these constraints are so strong that they completely describe the surfaces that can appear "generically". Before discussing what we mean by genericity, we cite a typical application of our results. We in fact place ourselves in the context of laminations, more general than that of foliations, where the ambient space is not assumed to be a manifold but an arbitrary compact metric space (see §2 for a definition).

Reference [2] on p. 422 is: "J. CANTWELL and L. CONLON, Leaf prescriptions for closed 3-manifolds, Trans. AMS 236 (1978), 239–261." [checked on the bibliography page below, §3.8].

### 3.2 Théorème A (p. 387, PDF 2) — verbatim, vision-verified

> THÉORÈME A. *Soit 𝓕 une lamination orientable de dimension 2 sur un espace compact quelconque. Si 𝓕 n'a pas de feuille compacte, alors une infinité non dénombrable de ses feuilles sont difféomorphes à l'une des six surfaces suivantes:*
> a) *le plan* **R**², 
> a') *le "monstre du Loch-Ness" i.e. le plan auquel on attache une infinité d'anses,*
> b) *le cylindre* S¹ × **R**,
> b') *l'"échelle de Jacob", i.e. le cylindre auquel on attache une infinité d'anses dans les deux directions,*
> c) *l'"arbre de Cantor", i.e. la sphère S² à laquelle on retire un ensemble de Cantor,*
> c') *l'"arbre de Cantor fleuri", i.e. l'arbre de Cantor auquel on attache une infinité d'anses dans toutes les directions.*

English:

> THEOREM A. *Let 𝓕 be an orientable lamination of dimension 2 on an arbitrary compact space. If 𝓕 has no compact leaf, then uncountably many of its leaves are diffeomorphic to one of the following six surfaces:*
> a) *the plane* **R**²,
> a') *the "Loch Ness monster", i.e. the plane with infinitely many handles attached,*
> b) *the cylinder* S¹ × **R**,
> b') *the "Jacob's ladder", i.e. the cylinder with infinitely many handles attached in both directions,*
> c) *the "Cantor tree", i.e. the sphere S² with a Cantor set removed,*
> c') *the "blooming Cantor tree", i.e. the Cantor tree with infinitely many handles attached in all directions.*

Figure 1 (p. 388, PDF 3) draws the six surfaces labeled a, a', b, b', c, c'. Immediately below it (p. 388):

> Dans le cas non orientable, on obtiendrait un résultat analogue en ajoutant trois surfaces à la liste précédente (voir §3).

> In the non-orientable case, one would obtain an analogous result by adding three surfaces to the preceding list (see §3).

Hypotheses of Theorem A, listed: lamination (not necessarily a foliation of a manifold); ambient space **compact** (any compact metric space); leaves of **dimension 2**; **orientable** (the six-surface list is for the orientable case; three more surfaces in the non-orientable case, §3); **no compact leaf**. No regularity hypothesis appears in the statement — the regularity of the lamination is fixed in §2 (see 3.6 below). Conclusion: *uncountably many* leaves are of one of the six types — NOT "every leaf", and NOT "almost every leaf" in the statement of A itself (that is Theorem A').

### 3.3 The notion of "generic" — Theorem A' (p. 388, PDF 3) — verbatim, vision-verified

The paragraph that fixes the meaning of "generic" (p. 388):

> Pour donner un sens à la notion de généricité, on est tout d'abord tenté d'utiliser une mesure transverse invariante par holonomie. Malheureusement, nos résultats ne pourraient pas alors s'appliquer à toutes les laminations puisque ce type de mesure n'existe que rarement. On utilise donc une notion plus générale, introduite par L. Garnett dans [7]. Soit 𝓕 une lamination sur un espace compact M et munissons le fibré tangent aux feuilles d'une métrique riemannienne (les conditions de régularité pour cette métrique sont très faibles, voir §2). Dans ces conditions, on peut privilégier certaines mesures sur M, appelées *mesures harmoniques* (par rapport à 𝓕 et à la métrique choisie). Ces mesures rendent compte de la distribution asymptotique d'un chemin brownien contenu dans une feuille de 𝓕. Nous rappellerons la définition et les principales propriétés de ces mesures au §2 mais il convient de remarquer dès maintenant que ces mesures ont l'avantage d'exister! Toute lamination possède au moins une mesure harmonique non triviale. Le résultat que nous montrons, plus précis que le théorème A, est le suivant:

> To give a meaning to the notion of genericity, one is first tempted to use a holonomy-invariant transverse measure. Unfortunately, our results could then not apply to all laminations, since this kind of measure exists only rarely. We therefore use a more general notion, introduced by L. Garnett in [7]. Let 𝓕 be a lamination on a compact space M and equip the tangent bundle to the leaves with a Riemannian metric (the regularity conditions on this metric are very weak, see §2). Under these conditions, one can single out certain measures on M, called *harmonic measures* (with respect to 𝓕 and the chosen metric). These measures account for the asymptotic distribution of a Brownian path contained in a leaf of 𝓕. We recall the definition and the main properties of these measures in §2, but it is worth remarking right away that these measures have the advantage of existing! Every lamination has at least one non-trivial harmonic measure. The result we prove, more precise than Theorem A, is the following:

> THÉORÈME A'. *Soit 𝓕 une lamination orientable de dimension 2 sur un espace compact. Alors, la réunion des feuilles qui sont compactes ou difféomorphes à l'une des six surfaces décrites plus haut est un borélien de mesure totale pour toute mesure harmonique (par rapport à 𝓕 et à n'importe quelle métrique le long des feuilles).*

> THEOREM A'. *Let 𝓕 be an orientable lamination of dimension 2 on a compact space. Then the union of the leaves that are compact or diffeomorphic to one of the six surfaces described above is a Borel set of full measure for every harmonic measure (with respect to 𝓕 and any metric along the leaves).*

**So "generic" in this paper is measure-theoretic with respect to harmonic measures (Garnett), NOT Baire-generic, and NOT with respect to a holonomy-invariant transverse measure** (which Ghys explicitly rejects as the definition because such measures "exist only rarely"). Since every holonomy-invariant transverse measure combined with leaf volume gives a harmonic measure (§2, see 3.6), the statement covers invariant measures as a special case.

### 3.4 The classification invariants and the characterization of the six surfaces (p. 389, PDF 4) — verbatim

> Le fait qu'il soit possible de décrire un nombre fini de surfaces qui apparaissent génériquement comme feuilles non compactes est bien entendu relié à la classification des surfaces. Rappelons que les surfaces, éventuellement non compactes, mais orientables pour simplifier, sont classées à difféomorphisme près par les invariants suivants [12]:
> i) le genre, qui est un entier ou +∞,
> ii) l'espace des bouts 𝓔 qui est un compact métrisable totalement discontinu quelconque (voir §3, ou [6], [13]),
> iii) l'ouvert 𝓔₀ ⊂ 𝓔 formé des *bouts planaires*, c'est-à-dire des bouts "ne portant pas d'anse". Si le genre est infini, on demande bien sûr que 𝓔₀ ≠ 𝓔 et, s'il est fini, on demande que 𝓔₀ = 𝓔.
> Les six surfaces que nous avons mentionnées sont caractérisées par le fait que 𝓔 est un ensemble à 1 ou 2 éléments ou est homéomorphe à un ensemble de Cantor, et que 𝓔₀ est soit vide soit égal à 𝓔.

> The fact that it is possible to describe finitely many surfaces that appear generically as non-compact leaves is of course related to the classification of surfaces. Recall that surfaces, possibly non-compact but orientable for simplicity, are classified up to diffeomorphism by the following invariants [12]:
> i) the genus, which is an integer or +∞,
> ii) the space of ends 𝓔, which is an arbitrary totally disconnected compact metrizable space (see §3, or [6], [13]),
> iii) the open set 𝓔₀ ⊂ 𝓔 of *planar ends*, i.e. ends "carrying no handle". If the genus is infinite one requires of course that 𝓔₀ ≠ 𝓔, and if it is finite one requires 𝓔₀ = 𝓔.
> The six surfaces we mentioned are characterized by the fact that 𝓔 is a set with 1 or 2 elements or is homeomorphic to a Cantor set, and that 𝓔₀ is either empty or equal to 𝓔.

So the definitions of the six types, read off from this and the list: a) plane = genus 0, one end; a') Loch Ness monster = genus ∞, one (non-planar) end; b) cylinder = genus 0, two ends; b') Jacob's ladder = genus ∞, two ends, both non-planar; c) Cantor tree = genus 0, ends a Cantor set; c') blooming Cantor tree = genus ∞, ends a Cantor set, all non-planar. (Reference [12] is Richards' classification; checked on p. 422 in 3.8.)

### 3.5 Théorème B (p. 389, PDF 4) — verbatim, vision-verified

> Evidemment, en dimension supérieure, une telle classification des variétés non compactes n'est pas concevable et une liste finie de feuilles génériques n'est plus possible. Pour illustrer ce fait, nous construisons au §1 un exemple d'une lamination de dimension 4 d'un espace compact dont toutes les feuilles sont denses et telle que deux feuilles distinctes quelconques sont non difféomorphes. Nous obtenons cependant le résultat suivant:
>
> THÉORÈME B. *Soit 𝓕 une lamination d'un espace compact M et μ une mesure harmonique (par rapport à une métrique quelconque le long des feuilles). Alors, la feuille de 𝓕 passant par μ-presque tout point de M a 0, 1, 2 ou un ensemble de Cantor de bouts.*

> Obviously, in higher dimension such a classification of non-compact manifolds is not conceivable and a finite list of generic leaves is no longer possible. To illustrate this, we construct in §1 an example of a lamination of dimension 4 of a compact space all of whose leaves are dense and such that any two distinct leaves are non-diffeomorphic. We nevertheless obtain the following result:
>
> THEOREM B. *Let 𝓕 be a lamination of a compact space M and μ a harmonic measure (with respect to an arbitrary metric along the leaves). Then the leaf of 𝓕 through μ-almost every point of M has 0, 1, 2 or a Cantor set of ends.*

Hypotheses: lamination of any dimension, compact ambient space, μ harmonic; conclusion is for μ-almost every point. Followed on p. 389 by the Hopf analogy ("un revêtement galoisien d'un polyèdre fini ne peut posséder que 0,1,2 ou un ensemble de Cantor de bouts") and the remark that the six surfaces are exactly the Galois coverings of compact orientable surfaces.

### 3.6 Théorème C (p. 390, PDF 5) — verbatim, vision-verified

> THÉORÈME C. *Soit 𝓕 une lamination sur un espace compact M et μ une mesure harmonique. Les conditions suivantes sont équivalentes:*
> i) *la feuille de 𝓕 passant par μ-presque tout point de M a deux bouts,*
> ii) *il existe un feuilletage mesuré 𝓖 de dimension 1 sur un espace borélien standard (X, 𝓐, m) et une application borélienne π: M → X telle que π_*μ = m et telle que la restriction de π à μ-presque toute feuille L de 𝓕 est une application continue et propre sur une feuille ℓ de 𝓖, envoyant les deux bouts de L sur les deux bouts de ℓ.*

> THEOREM C. *Let 𝓕 be a lamination on a compact space M and μ a harmonic measure. The following conditions are equivalent:*
> i) *the leaf of 𝓕 through μ-almost every point of M has two ends,*
> ii) *there exist a measured foliation 𝓖 of dimension 1 on a standard Borel space (X, 𝓐, m) and a Borel map π: M → X such that π_*μ = m and such that the restriction of π to μ-almost every leaf L of 𝓕 is a continuous proper map onto a leaf ℓ of 𝓖, sending the two ends of L to the two ends of ℓ.*

(The notion "feuilletage mesuré de dimension 1" is defined on p. 390 just above: orbits of an aperiodic measure-preserving measurable flow on a standard Borel space, or its quotient by a measurable fixed-point-free involution conjugating φ^t to φ^{−t}, for the non-oriented version.)

> THÉORÈME D. *Soit 𝓕 une lamination sur un espace compact et μ une mesure harmonique ergodique. Les conditions suivantes sont équivalentes:*
> i) *la feuille passant par μ-presque tout point a au moins deux bouts,*
> ii) *le pseudogroupe transverse mesurable associé à μ est équivalent au sens de Kakutani à une HNN-extension non triviale.*

> THEOREM D. *Let 𝓕 be a lamination on a compact space and μ an ergodic harmonic measure. The following conditions are equivalent:*
> i) *the leaf through μ-almost every point has at least two ends,*
> ii) *the measurable transverse pseudogroup associated with μ is Kakutani-equivalent to a non-trivial HNN-extension.*

("HNN-extension non triviale" of a measurable pseudogroup is defined on p. 391; "ergodique" on p. 391: every saturated Borel set has μ-measure zero or full.) The intro closes (p. 392) with the open problem of which *quasi-isometry types* of the six surfaces occur generically, citing [5] = D. Cass, *Minimal leaves in foliations*, Trans. AMS 287 (1985) 201–213, for "quelques informations sur les types de quasi-isométries des feuilles apparaissant dans une lamination dont toutes les feuilles sont denses" (i.e. a minimal lamination). Ghys also acknowledges Dennis Sullivan's remark "there is no measurable way to pick a point in a leaf" as the origin of the work.

### 3.8 Structure of the paper, regularity hypotheses, and the two ingredients the program cares about

**Sections** (from the roadmap on p. 392): §1 examples (pp. 392–399); §2 harmonic measures and the "proposition fondamentale" (pp. 399–405); §3 proof of Theorems A and B (pp. 405–411); §4 laminations with "two ends" or "a Cantor set of ends", i.e. Theorems C and D (pp. 411–420); §5 measurability of the Borel sets used (pp. 420–422). Bibliography p. 422.

**Definition of lamination and the regularity hypothesis (p. 400, PDF 15) — verbatim:**

> On se fixe un espace métrisable compact séparable M muni d'une lamination 𝓕 de dimension d. Plus précisément, nous supposons M recouvert par un nombre fini d'ouverts U_i homéomorphes à un ouvert de **R**^d × T_i (où T_i est un certain espace localement compact), de sorte que les "changements de cartes" aient la forme suivante sur leur domaine de définition:
> (x, y) ∈ **R**^d × T_i ↦ (F_y(x), g(y)) ∈ **R**^d × T_j.
> Ici, g est un homéomorphisme et F_y est un difféomorphisme que nous supposerons de classe C³, dépendant continûment de y dans la topologie C³. Nous fixerons par ailleurs une métrique riemannienne le long des feuilles de 𝓕 dont on supposera qu'elle est donnée, dans chaque carte U_i par une métrique de classe C³ sur (un ouvert de) **R**^d dépendant continûment du paramètre y dans la topologie C³. Ces hypothèses de régularité sont très faibles.
> Des exemples de laminations sont fournis par la restriction d'un feuilletage à un fermé invariant mais beaucoup de laminations ne sont pas naturellement plongées dans une variété.

> We fix a compact separable metrizable space M with a lamination 𝓕 of dimension d. More precisely, we assume M covered by finitely many open sets U_i homeomorphic to an open subset of **R**^d × T_i (T_i some locally compact space), such that the "changes of charts" have the following form on their domain: (x, y) ↦ (F_y(x), g(y)). Here g is a homeomorphism and F_y is a diffeomorphism which we assume of class C³, depending continuously on y in the C³ topology. We further fix a Riemannian metric along the leaves of 𝓕, assumed to be given in each chart U_i by a C³ metric on (an open subset of) **R**^d depending continuously on the parameter y in the C³ topology. These regularity hypotheses are very weak.
> Examples of laminations are provided by the restriction of a foliation to a closed invariant set, but many laminations are not naturally embedded in a manifold.

So the standing hypotheses of Theorems A, A', B, C, D are: **compact** metrizable ambient space; leaves **C³ along the leaves** (transversally only continuous — C⁰ in the transverse parameter); a leafwise Riemannian metric C³ along leaves, continuous transversally. **No codimension hypothesis** (the transversal T_i is any locally compact space) and **no C² transverse regularity** — in particular a C² codimension-one foliation of a closed 3-manifold restricted to any closed saturated set (e.g. a minimal set) is a lamination in this sense, so every theorem in the paper applies to the restricted lamination.

**Harmonic measures vs invariant transverse measures (p. 401, PDF 16) — verbatim:**

> Plaçons nous dans le cas particulier où 𝓕 possède une mesure transverse invariante par holonomie. On peut alors combiner cette mesure avec le volume le long des feuilles pour obtenir une mesure globale μ sur M. Le fait que la mesure transverse soit invariante par holonomie garantit que μ est bien définie. Evidemment, si l'on désintègre μ dans un ouvert U_i, les mesures λ_y seront des multiples constants du volume des feuilles. Le théorème précédent montre alors que μ est harmonique. Ainsi, les mesures transverses invariantes par holonomie produisent des cas particuliers de mesures harmoniques, qui seront appelées *complètement invariantes*.

> Consider the particular case where 𝓕 has a holonomy-invariant transverse measure. One can then combine this measure with the volume along the leaves to obtain a global measure μ on M. Holonomy invariance of the transverse measure guarantees that μ is well defined. Obviously, if one disintegrates μ in an open set U_i, the measures λ_y are constant multiples of leaf volume. The preceding theorem then shows that μ is harmonic. Thus holonomy-invariant transverse measures produce special cases of harmonic measures, which will be called *completely invariant*.

This matters for the program because an S4′ object carries a holonomy-invariant transverse measure automatically (adjudication A-III): the associated completely invariant harmonic measure is one to which Theorems A', B apply.

**Proposition fondamentale (p. 402, PDF 17) — verbatim:**

> PROPOSITION FONDAMENTALE. *Soit 𝓕 une lamination sur un espace compact M, munie d'une métrique le long des feuilles et soit μ une mesure harmonique quelconque. Soit B ⊂ M un borélien. Alors, pour μ-presque tout point x de M, l'intersection L_x ∩ B de B et de la feuille L_x passant par x est ou bien vide ou bien s'approche de tous les bouts de L_x. Plus précisément, dans le second cas, si V ⊂ L_x est un domaine non compact à frontière ∂V (= V̄ − int V) compacte, alors V rencontre B.*

> FUNDAMENTAL PROPOSITION. *Let 𝓕 be a lamination on a compact space M with a metric along the leaves, and let μ be any harmonic measure. Let B ⊂ M be a Borel set. Then for μ-almost every point x of M, the intersection L_x ∩ B of B with the leaf L_x through x is either empty or approaches every end of L_x. More precisely, in the second case, if V ⊂ L_x is a non-compact domain with compact boundary ∂V (= V̄ − int V), then V meets B.*

This is the "quasi-periodicity" engine; it is a μ-a.e. statement and nothing in the paper upgrades it to "every leaf".

### 3.9 The proof that A' ⟹ A, and the non-orientable list (pp. 410–411, PDF 25–26) — verbatim

Conclusion of the proof of A' (p. 410):

> Nous avons donc montré que la feuille passant par μ-presque tout point est ou bien de genre nul (et tous ses bouts sont alors planaires) ou bien tous ses bouts sont non planaires. Le théorème B associé au théorème de classification des surfaces non compactes (orientables) entraîne le théorème A'.
> Il faut encore justifier le fait que le théorème A' entraîne le théorème A. Il s'agit de montrer qu'une mesure harmonique concentrée sur une réunion dénombrable de feuilles ne peut se concentrer que sur des feuilles compactes. Supposons en effet qu'une feuille L soit de mesure positive. La description locale des mesures harmoniques montre alors qu'une boule compacte B contenue [p. 411] dans L est de mesure positive. La proposition fondamentale, appliquée à B, entraîne que le compact B s'approche de tous les bouts de L. C'est donc que L n'a pas de bout; c'est une feuille compacte.

> We have thus shown that the leaf through μ-almost every point is either of genus zero (and all its ends are then planar) or all its ends are non-planar. Theorem B together with the classification theorem for non-compact (orientable) surfaces implies Theorem A'.
> It remains to justify that Theorem A' implies Theorem A. One must show that a harmonic measure concentrated on a countable union of leaves can only be concentrated on compact leaves. Suppose a leaf L has positive measure. The local description of harmonic measures then shows that a compact ball B contained in L has positive measure. The fundamental proposition applied to B implies that the compact set B approaches every end of L. Hence L has no end; it is a compact leaf.

**So Theorem A's "uncountably many" is exactly what one gets from "a.e. for every harmonic measure" plus "no harmonic measure charges a non-compact leaf"; the paper proves nothing about every leaf.**

Non-orientable case (p. 411):

> Par conséquent, si l'on ne suppose plus la lamination de dimension 2 orientable dans les théorèmes A, A', il faut ajouter à la liste des six surfaces a), a'), b), b'), c), c') les trois surfaces non orientables suivantes:
> a'') le "monstre du Loch Ness non orientable",
> b'') l'"échelle de Jacob non orientable",
> c'') l'"arbre de Cantor non orientable",
> obtenus respectivement à partir du plan, du cylindre et de l'arbre de Cantor en faisant des sommes connexes avec une infinité de plans projectifs s'approchant de tous les bouts.

> Consequently, if one no longer assumes the 2-dimensional lamination orientable in Theorems A, A', one must add to the list of six surfaces a), a'), b), b'), c), c') the following three non-orientable surfaces: a'') the "non-orientable Loch Ness monster", b'') the "non-orientable Jacob's ladder", c'') the "non-orientable Cantor tree", obtained respectively from the plane, the cylinder and the Cantor tree by connected sums with infinitely many projective planes approaching all the ends.

(The invariants used there: 𝓔 ⊃ 𝓔_np ⊃ 𝓔_nor, ends, non-planar ends, non-orientable ends; for μ-a.e. x, 𝓔(L_x) has 0, 1, 2 elements or is a Cantor set, 𝓔_np(L_x) is empty or all of 𝓔(L_x), 𝓔_nor(L_x) is empty or all of 𝓔_np(L_x).) The program's leaves are Riemann surfaces, hence orientable, so the six-surface list is the relevant one.

### 3.10 What the paper says about NON-generic leaves and about minimal sets — all passages, verbatim

The words "ensemble minimal" occur in the paper only in §1 (pp. 396–398), in the construction of a counterexample. The theorem statements A–D never mention minimal sets. Here is everything relevant.

**(i) Plane and cylinder as leaves of a minimal foliation — every leaf (p. 393, PDF 8):**

> Les cas du *plan* et du *cylindre* ne posent aucune difficulté. Un feuilletage linéaire de dimension 2 sur un tore de dimension 3 peut avoir *toutes* ses feuilles difféomorphes à un plan ou *toutes* ses feuilles difféomorphes à un cylindre.

> The cases of the *plane* and of the *cylinder* present no difficulty. A linear foliation of dimension 2 on a 3-torus can have *all* its leaves diffeomorphic to a plane or *all* its leaves diffeomorphic to a cylinder.

**(ii) Cantor tree: Hirsch's example, and an explicit admission of ignorance about "all leaves" (p. 393):**

> Le cas de l'*arbre de Cantor* se rencontre par exemple dans un feuilletage construit par M. Hirsch. Dans un tore solide D² × S¹, on retire l'intérieur d'un tore solide, voisinage d'une tresse (voir figure 2). On obtient ainsi une variété de dimension 3, à bord, dont le bord est constitué de deux tores T². Cette variété est naturellement feuilletée par "pantalons" (sphère moins trois disques) transverses au bord. En recollant les deux composantes connexes du bord par un difféomorphisme convenable, on obtient une variété fermée de dimension 3 munie d'un feuilletage de codimension 1. Les feuilles de ce feuilletage sont toutes des "arbres de Cantor" sauf celles qui correspondent aux "points périodiques" du recollement. Il est naturellement possible de supposer que le nombre de feuilles du second type est dénombrable de sorte que la feuille générique est effectivement un arbre de Cantor. Nous ne connaissons pas d'exemple de feuilletage d'une variété fermée de dimension 3 dont *toutes* les feuilles sont de tels arbres de Cantor.

> The case of the *Cantor tree* occurs for instance in a foliation constructed by M. Hirsch. In a solid torus D² × S¹, remove the interior of a solid torus that is a neighborhood of a braid (see Figure 2). One obtains a 3-manifold with boundary consisting of two tori T². This manifold is naturally foliated by "pairs of pants" (sphere minus three disks) transverse to the boundary. Gluing the two boundary components by a suitable diffeomorphism, one obtains a closed 3-manifold with a codimension-one foliation. The leaves of this foliation are all "Cantor trees" except those corresponding to the "periodic points" of the gluing. One can naturally arrange that the number of leaves of the second kind is countable, so that the generic leaf is indeed a Cantor tree. We know of no example of a foliation of a closed 3-manifold *all* of whose leaves are such Cantor trees.

**(iii) Loch Ness monsters, Jacob's ladders, blooming Cantor trees — every leaf (p. 394, PDF 9):** after the "adding genus" construction (remove a tubular neighborhood of a closed transversal, glue in S × S¹ with S a genus-2 surface with one boundary component, foliated by S × {∗}):

> En appliquant cette construction aux feuilletages que nous venons de décrire, on obtient des *exemples dont toutes les feuilles sont respectivement des monstres du Loch-Ness, des échelles de Jacob et des arbres de Cantor fleuris*. En ce qui concerne le dernier cas, le lecteur vérifiera en effet que l'adjonction d'anses identifie les feuilles génériques et spéciales du feuilletage de Hirsch.

> Applying this construction to the foliations just described, one obtains *examples all of whose leaves are respectively Loch Ness monsters, Jacob's ladders and blooming Cantor trees*. As for the last case, the reader will check that adding handles identifies the generic and the special leaves of Hirsch's foliation.

**(iv) The non-generic leaves are unconstrained — the example 𝓕'' (p. 394):**

> Nous construisons maintenant un exemple illustrant la signification de la généricité utilisée dans cet article. Soit 𝓕 un feuilletage linéaire par plans sur le tore T³ et c₁, c₂ deux courbes fermées simples disjointes et transverses à 𝓕. Soit 𝓕' le feuilletage sur T³ obtenu par tourbillonnement autour de c₁ et c₂ i.e. en introduisant des composantes de Reeb aux voisinages de c₁ et c₂. Les feuilles de 𝓕' extérieures à ces composantes ont une géométrie suggérée par la figure 3. Si l'on ôte à T³ l'intérieur des deux composantes de Reeb de 𝓕' et si l'on identifie les deux composantes toriques du bord qui en résultent, on obtient un feuilletage 𝓕'' sur une variété fermée M qui possède exactement une feuille compacte. Les autres feuilles sont toutes difféomorphes à un plan privé d'un ensemble discret dénombrable. Notons que 𝓕'' est ergodique par rapport à la mesure de Lebesgue de M puisque le feuilletage original 𝓕 sur T³ l'est. Par conséquent, un feuilletage peut être ergodique par rapport à la mesure de Lebesgue sans que la plupart de ses feuilles ne soient difféomorphes à l'une des six surfaces du théorème A. Cet exemple montre qu'*il est sans espoir de vouloir généraliser le théorème B aux bouts des orbites d'un pseudogroupe mesurable pour une mesure quasi-invariante ergodique*. Bien sûr, il résulte du théorème B que, dans cet exemple, l'unique mesure harmonique de 𝓕'' se concentre sur la feuille compacte.

> We now construct an example illustrating the meaning of the genericity used in this article. Let 𝓕 be a linear foliation by planes on the torus T³ and c₁, c₂ two disjoint simple closed curves transverse to 𝓕. Let 𝓕' be the foliation on T³ obtained by turbulization around c₁ and c₂, i.e. by introducing Reeb components in neighborhoods of c₁ and c₂. The leaves of 𝓕' outside these components have the geometry suggested by Figure 3. If one removes from T³ the interior of the two Reeb components of 𝓕' and identifies the two resulting boundary tori, one obtains a foliation 𝓕'' on a closed manifold M which has exactly one compact leaf. The other leaves are all diffeomorphic to a plane minus a countable discrete set. Note that 𝓕'' is ergodic with respect to Lebesgue measure on M since the original foliation 𝓕 on T³ is. Consequently, a foliation can be ergodic with respect to Lebesgue measure without most of its leaves being diffeomorphic to one of the six surfaces of Theorem A. This example shows that *it is hopeless to try to generalize Theorem B to the ends of the orbits of a measurable pseudogroup with an ergodic quasi-invariant measure*. Of course, it follows from Theorem B that in this example the unique harmonic measure of 𝓕'' is concentrated on the compact leaf.

(p. 395 continues with a variant 𝓕''' whose generic leaves have an end space that is *not* a Cantor set — "L'espace des bouts de l'orbite générique d'une HNN-extension non triviale peut donc être infini sans être un ensemble de Cantor" — again for a quasi-invariant, non-harmonic measure; and an example on a closed 5-manifold with an invariant transverse volume where "presque toutes les feuilles ont deux bouts mais dont certaines en ont un nombre dénombrable ou fini supérieur à 2".)

**(v) A minimal set whose leaves are pairwise non-homeomorphic (p. 396, PDF 11) — the one theorem about minimal sets, verbatim:**

> THÉORÈME. *Il existe un feuilletage 𝓕 sur une variété compacte M possédant un ensemble minimal 𝓜 non réduit à une feuille compacte et tel que deux feuilles différentes quelconques dans 𝓜 sont non homéomorphes.*

> THEOREM. *There exists a foliation 𝓕 on a compact manifold M having a minimal set 𝓜 not reduced to a compact leaf and such that any two distinct leaves in 𝓜 are non-homeomorphic.*

The construction (pp. 396–399): a Smale-horseshoe diffeomorphism φ of S² with invariant Cantor set Λ carrying the full shift on {0,1}^**Z**; two closed orientable 4-manifolds V₀, V₁ with π₁ ≅ **Z**³, **Z**⁴; a codimension-2 foliation on a closed 6-manifold M = M̄/(φ × id), whose leaves over a point of Λ with itinerary (ε_i) are the infinite connected sums L_(ε_i) of V'_0, V'_1 glued along S² × S¹; distinguished by the amalgamated-sum structure of π₁ (Bass–Serre theory, [13], [14]). p. 397: "d'autant que nous sommes essentiellement intéressés par la construction d'une lamination 𝓜 et beaucoup moins par celle d'un feuilletage sur une variété dont 𝓜 est un ensemble minimal." p. 398: "à un ensemble minimal pour φ contenu dans Λ correspond un ensemble minimal pour 𝓕." Closing remarks (p. 399):

> L'exemple que nous venons de construire est un feuilletage de codimension 2 sur une variété de dimension 6. Il est probablement possible de construire un exemple sur une variété de dimension 4, en codimension 1, en se fondant sur la même idée. Par contre, le théorème A montre qu'un tel exemple ne peut exister avec des feuilles de dimension 2.
> Nous ignorons s'il existe un *feuilletage* d'une variété *compacte* tel que deux feuilles différentes quelconques sont non homéomorphes.

> The example just constructed is a codimension-2 foliation on a 6-manifold. It is probably possible to construct an example on a 4-manifold, in codimension 1, based on the same idea. On the other hand, Theorem A shows that no such example can exist with leaves of dimension 2.
> We do not know whether there is a *foliation* of a *compact* manifold such that any two distinct leaves are non-homeomorphic.

**Answer to the (c) question — does the paper constrain ALL leaves of a minimal set or only almost all?** Only almost all, and only with respect to harmonic measures (which include the completely invariant ones built from invariant transverse measures). Precisely:
- The theorems are about "μ-presque tout point" for harmonic μ (A', B, C, D) or, as a corollary, "une infinité non dénombrable de feuilles" (A). No theorem in the paper says anything about every leaf, and the paper's own examples (𝓕'' on p. 394: all leaves but one are a plane minus a countable discrete set, not on the list) show the non-generic leaves are not constrained by its methods.
- Because a minimal set 𝓜 of a foliation is a compact lamination in the sense of p. 400 ("la restriction d'un feuilletage à un fermé invariant"), Theorems A, A', B apply to 𝓕|𝓜 — I infer, directly from the definition: **if 𝓜 is a minimal set of a 2-dimensional orientable lamination and 𝓜 is not a compact leaf, then 𝓜 has no compact leaf (a compact leaf in a minimal set is the whole minimal set), so uncountably many leaves of 𝓜 are among the six surfaces, and for every harmonic measure supported on 𝓜, a.e. leaf of 𝓜 is one of the six.** That is the strongest minimal-set statement obtainable from this paper, and it still says nothing about any particular leaf.
- The paper's own exhibit of a minimal set (p. 396) is one in which *no two leaves are homeomorphic* — the exact opposite of "every leaf of a minimal set has the same topology" — though with 4-dimensional leaves, where Theorem A does not apply.

### 3.11 Relation to Cantwell–Conlon and Duminy

- **Duminy is not mentioned anywhere in the paper** (text-layer search for "Dumin" returns nothing; bibliography p. 422 read by vision, 17 entries, none is Duminy). The words "semi-propre"/"semiproper" and "exceptionnel" (exceptional minimal set) do not occur either.
- **Cantwell–Conlon** appears three times: [2] *Leaf prescriptions for closed 3-manifolds*, Trans. AMS 236 (1978) 239–261, quoted on p. 387 as the theorem that any non-compact surface is a leaf of some 2-dimensional foliation on any closed 3-manifold (Ghys's foil: "la topologie de la 'plupart' de ses feuilles est soumise à de sérieuses contraintes"); [3] *Endsets of leaves*, Topology 21 (1982) 333–352; [4] *Every surface is a leaf*, Topology 26 (1987) 265–285; [2],[3] cited on p. 394 for "de nombreux exemples de types d'isométrie possibles". No statement in the paper is attributed to Cantwell–Conlon beyond these.
- The only place the paper's result *touches* Duminy's is Theorem B: harmonic-a.e. leaf has 0, 1, 2 or a Cantor set of ends. Duminy's theorem (as the program has it from Hurder's problem set) says every *semiproper* leaf of an *exceptional minimal set* of a C² codimension-one foliation has a Cantor set of ends — a statement about specific leaves, with a transverse-regularity hypothesis, in codimension one, excluding the 1- and 2-end cases. Ghys's is about a.e. leaf, any codimension, transversally C⁰, and it *permits* one end (plane, Loch Ness monster) and two ends (cylinder, Jacob's ladder) generically. **The two results are logically independent and neither cites the other.** I infer: where both apply (exceptional minimal set of a C² codimension-one foliation, harmonic measure supported on it, semiproper leaves of positive measure), they are consistent — Duminy forces the Cantor-set option among Ghys's three.

### 3.12 Plain-English reading for §5 step 2 (what this settles, what it does not)

**The source says:**
1. In any compact 2-dimensional orientable lamination (any compact metric space, leaves C³, transversally continuous; a minimal set of a C² codimension-one foliation of a closed 3-manifold qualifies), for every harmonic measure — in particular for the completely invariant one built from any holonomy-invariant transverse measure — almost every leaf is compact or one of six surfaces: plane, Loch Ness monster, cylinder, Jacob's ladder, Cantor tree, blooming Cantor tree (Theorem A', p. 388). If there is no compact leaf, uncountably many leaves are of these six types (Theorem A, p. 387).
2. "Generic" means harmonic-measure-a.e. (Garnett), explicitly *not* Baire and explicitly not restricted to invariant transverse measures (p. 388).
3. **The plane is on the list.** Nothing in the paper forbids a plane leaf, generic or not, in a minimal set or anywhere else; on the contrary the linear foliation of T³ has *every* leaf a plane (p. 393) and is minimal.
4. The paper never constrains a single named leaf. Its own example 𝓕'' (p. 394) has every non-compact leaf a plane minus a countable discrete set — not on the list — which the paper says is exactly what its notion of genericity is designed to ignore.
5. The paper proves nothing about which surfaces occur as leaves of a minimal set beyond what (1) gives by restriction; its one theorem about a minimal set (p. 396) exhibits a minimal set with pairwise non-homeomorphic leaves (leaves of dimension 4).
6. Duminy, semiproper leaves and exceptional minimal sets do not appear.

**Therefore, for Q-S4⁗:**
- **(a) — the lamination question — is NOT decided by this paper, in either direction.** Ghys 1995 gives no obstruction to a plane leaf L in a compact saturated sublamination N (the plane is one of the six generic types), and it says nothing about flows, non-transverse sets, or scaling of transverse measures. The adjudication's hope that this paper "decide[s] (a) for laminations, or say[s] that it is open" is not borne out: the paper is silent on (a). What it does contribute is a *consistency check that passes*: an S4′ object's N carries a holonomy-invariant transverse measure (A-III), hence a completely invariant harmonic measure, and the plane leaf is allowed by Theorem A' for that measure. (I infer; the paper does not discuss anything like N.)
- **(b) — the closed-3-manifold question — is NOT decided by this paper either.** The paper contains no theorem forbidding a plane leaf in a compact saturated set that is not a finite union of compact leaves. Two remarks, both "I infer" from the text of p. 393: (i) read literally, (b) is answered YES by the irrational linear foliation of T³, every leaf of which is a flat plane, i.e. conformally C, lying in the compact saturated set T³ itself; the program presumably intends N to be a proper subset / an exceptional minimal set, in which case (ii) this paper does not address it, and the operative instrument remains Duminy's theorem (semiproper leaves of an exceptional minimal set have a Cantor set of ends) plus Hurder's Problem 5.4 — exactly as §5 step 1 already says. Ghys 1995 neither strengthens nor weakens that route.
- **What it rules out for the *typical* leaf of N, if the program ever needs it:** for any harmonic measure on N (in particular the invariant one), the a.e. leaf of N is one of the six; so if the program's construction wanted, say, *all* leaves of N to be planes minus a countable set, or surfaces with a countably infinite end space, Theorem A' forbids that for a.e. leaf. It does not forbid the single plane leaf L.
- **Net for §5 step 2:** the item should be recorded as READ, with the verdict "silent on (a) and (b); no obstruction to a plane leaf; genericity is harmonic-measure-a.e., never every-leaf; Duminy absent." The manifold half of Q-S4⁗ is decided, if at all, by item 1 (Cantwell–Conlon/Duminy) and not by this paper. The companion fetch in step 2, Ghys 1999 *Laminations par surfaces de Riemann* (r3s-35 / r4-07), is where the compact-Riemann-surface-lamination version lives; this 1995 paper cites Candel's uniformization [1] but does not treat conformal type ("Le plan, par exemple, peut être un plan euclidien, un plan hyperbolique...", p. 392, left as an open direction).

**Nothing in this source contradicts anything on the program's record.** The adjudication's description of the paper ("whose subject is exactly which surfaces occur as leaves in a minimal set") is slightly off: the subject is which surfaces occur as *harmonic-a.e.* leaves of a *compact lamination*, minimal or not; the phrase "in a minimal set" should be read as "in the support of a harmonic measure". This is a wording nit, not a contradiction — recorded here per the brief, not fixed in the adjudication.

## §4. Other things in the source a future session should know exist

- p. 389: Hopf's theorem (a Galois covering of a finite polyhedron has 0, 1, 2 or a Cantor set of ends) as the model for Theorem B; the six surfaces are exactly the Galois coverings of compact orientable surfaces.
- p. 390: definition of "feuilletage mesuré de dimension 1" (measurable flows on standard Borel spaces, and the non-oriented quotient); Theorem C = "laminations with two ends are compact extensions of 1-dimensional laminations".
- p. 391: measurable pseudogroups, holonomy pseudogroup of a lamination, Kakutani equivalence, non-trivial HNN-extension, ergodicity of a harmonic measure.
- p. 392: open problem — which *quasi-isometry* types of the six surfaces are generic; cites Cass [5] *Minimal leaves in foliations*, Trans. AMS 287 (1985) 201–213 for laminations with all leaves dense (i.e. minimal laminations) — a possibly useful reference for the program's minimal-set questions, not on disk.
- p. 394: the stable foliation of the geodesic flow of a closed surface of curvature −1 has almost all leaves planar and isometric to the Poincaré disk (a hyperbolic-plane example, relevant to the "conformal to C" vs "conformal to D" distinction).
- p. 395: a closed 5-manifold example with an invariant transverse volume in which a.e. leaf has two ends (Jacob's ladder) but some leaves have any finite even number 2 + 2k or infinitely many ends (k ∈ N ∪ {∞}); non-generic leaves with countably many ends coexist with an invariant measure.
- p. 400: Candel [1] and Sullivan [16] cited for laminations not embedded in manifolds.
- p. 401 (Theorem [7], Garnett): local structure of harmonic measures — disintegration along leaves has densities that are positive harmonic functions; for 1-dimensional foliations every harmonic measure is completely invariant.
- p. 402: harmonic measures via Wiener measure / Brownian motion; Theorem [7] (μ harmonic iff μ̄ is S_τ-invariant).
- pp. 405–410: the discrete version of the fundamental proposition on the graph P̄(x) of the holonomy pseudogroup and the Borel sets **B**_k (measurability proved in §5, pp. 420–422).
- pp. 411–420: proofs of Theorems C, D; "lemme de l'hypersurface" (p. ~412) and a lemma that an ergodic harmonic measure that is not completely invariant admits a non-constant positive (super)harmonic function on a.e. leaf; Theorem D via HNN-extensions.
- p. 422: received September 27, 1993; author at École Normale Supérieure de Lyon.
