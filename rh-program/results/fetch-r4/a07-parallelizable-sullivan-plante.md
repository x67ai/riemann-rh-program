## §3 The answer from the sources

### Item 13 — Dugundji–Antosiewicz 1961 (all quotations verified by vision on rendered pages; ℱ denotes the flow, R the metric space, E the real line, f: R × E → R the flow map)

**Setting (printed p. 543, §1):** "Let ℱ be a flow on an arbitrary metric space R; i.e., ℱ is a continuous map f: R × E → R (where E is the real line) such that f(p, 0) = p and f(f(p, t), t′) = f(p, t + t′) whatever p ∈ R and t, t′ ∈ E."

**Definition of section (p. 544, Definition 1):** "We recall the following definition due to Whitney [9] (cf., also, [7]). DEFINITION 1. *A set S ⊂ R is a section of ℱ if for every p ∈ R there exists a unique t_s(p) ∈ E such that f(p, −t_s(p)) ∈ S.* Clearly, ℱ has a section if and only if it has no closed orbits and no rest points.¹ Thus t_s: R → E is 1–1 along every orbit of ℱ. The function t_s(p) will be basic throughout the sequel. In general, it need not be continuous on R and so S can be a "pathological" set."

**Definition of parallelizable (p. 544, Definition 2):** "DEFINITION 2. *ℱ is parallelizable if there exists a set S ⊂ R and a homeomorphism h of R onto S × E such that f(S, E) = R and h(f(p, t)) = (p, t) for every p ∈ S, t ∈ E.* Note that S must be closed because h^{−1}(S × {0}) = S."

**The characterization "in the large" (p. 544, Theorem 1):** "THEOREM 1. *ℱ is parallelizable if and only if it has a section S with t_s continuous on R.* PROOF. Necessity is clear. To prove sufficiency, define φ: R → S × E by φ(p) = (f(p, −t_s(p)), t_s(p)) and observe that φ is continuous, 1–1 and onto on R. Since its inverse is ψ(s, t) = f(s, t), s ∈ S, t ∈ E, which is continuous, the assertion follows."

**Definition of dispersive (pp. 544–545):** "Definition 2 implies that a parallelizable flow on any metric space R is dispersive [6]: for every two points p, q ∈ R there exists neighborhoods U, V and a constant T > 0 such that f(U, t) ∩ V = ∅ for every |t| > T. We shall prove that, conversely, if R is locally compact and separable, a dispersive flow on R is parallelizable." ([6] = Montgomery–Zippin, *Translation groups of three-space*, Amer. J. Math. 59 (1937) 121–128.)

**Wandering point, completely unstable, unstable, improper saddle point (pp. 545–546):** "We can prove more if q ∈ R is a wandering point of ℱ; i.e., if there exists a neighborhood V of q and a constant T > 0 such that f(V, t) ∩ V = ∅ for |t| > T." "LEMMA 3. *If R is locally compact and q ∈ R is a wandering point of ℱ, there exists a set S ⊂ R containing q such that U = f(S, E) is open and S is an (∞, U)-section of ℱ with t_s continuous on U.*" "THEOREM 2. *If R is locally compact and separable and if every p ∈ R is a wandering point of ℱ, there exists a countable covering {T(L_n)} of R by compactly-based tubes such that each t_{L_n} is continuous on T(L_n).* A flow on a locally compact metric space R for which every p ∈ R is a wandering point is called completely unstable [7]. Such a flow is a fortiori unstable.³" Footnote 3: "A flow on a locally compact metric space R is said to be unstable if, for every p ∈ R, neither the positive nor the negative semi-orbit through p is contained in a compact set." Footnote 4: "A flow on a locally compact metric space R is said to have an improper saddle point if there exist p_n ∈ R and divergent increasing sequences {t_n}, {τ_n} of constants t_n > τ_n > 0 such that p_n → p, f(p_n, t_n) → q and {f(p_n, τ_n)} contains no convergent subsequence." (p. 546) "Lemma 3 together with Theorem 1 shows that a completely unstable flow is parallelizable on an (open) ∞-tube around every p ∈ R. However, in general, it is not parallelizable on R, even when R = Eⁿ."

**The main theorem (p. 548, Theorem 3):** "THEOREM 3. *A flow ℱ on a locally compact separable metric space R is parallelizable if and only if it is dispersive.* PROOF. Only the sufficiency part requires proof. By Theorem 1 it suffices to show that ℱ has a section S with t_s continuous on R. …" followed by: "It follows that the concepts of dispersive flow and unstable flow with no improper saddle point are equivalent for locally compact separable metric spaces."

**What the paper does NOT contain.** The phrase "time function" does not occur anywhere in the paper (text-layer search, confirmed on the rendered pages 543–548). The paper's datum is the pair (section S, the function t_s: R → E with f(p, −t_s(p)) ∈ S), and t_s does satisfy t_s(f(p, t)) = t_s(p) + t (this is immediate from the definition, since f(f(p,t), −t_s(p) − t) = f(p, −t_s(p)) ∈ S and uniqueness), but the paper never states that identity or names t_s. Nor does the paper anywhere state "parallelizable ⟺ all limit sets empty" or "⟺ no nonempty compact invariant set"; its equivalences are parallelizable ⟺ section with t_s continuous (Thm 1, any metric space) and parallelizable ⟺ dispersive ⟺ unstable with no improper saddle point (Thm 3 and the sentence after it, locally compact separable metric spaces). Part II (pp. 548–555) is about Lyapunov functions for the flow on Eⁿ × E of a nonautonomous ODE; its Theorem 4 (p. 550) constructs, with no stability hypothesis, a continuous V with 0 ≤ V ≤ 1 and ∂V/∂t ≤ 0 along orbits — a monotone, not a strictly additive, function — and Theorem 6 (p. 552) gives ∂V/∂t ≤ −V (exponential decay along orbits) under uniform asymptotic stability. The closing remark (p. 555) is the smooth version: "Suppose ℱ is a dispersive flow on an n-dimensional separable manifold R of class C^m (m ≥ 1), the flow being defined by a map f: R × E → R of class C^k, 1 ≤ k ≤ m. Then ℱ has a (global) section S ⊂ R such that, for every p ∈ S, there exists a real valued function φ_p of class C^k on R and a neighborhood U_p of p for which U_p ⊂ φ_p^{−1}(0)."

**Hájek 1971.** Not available (see §1, 13b). Nothing can be quoted from it.

**Is the Session-14 sentence accurately worded?** Judged against the primary source now in hand:

1. *"The existence of such a function [f with f(φ^t x) = t + f(x)] is the classical characterization of a parallelizable flow"* — **accurate in substance, inaccurate as attribution.** Dugundji–Antosiewicz's Theorem 1 characterizes parallelizability by a section S with **t_s continuous on R**; t_s is exactly such a function (continuous, real-valued, additive along orbits: t_s(f(p,t)) = t_s(p) + t), and conversely a continuous additive f gives the section S = f^{−1}(0). So "there is a continuous f: R → ℝ with f(φ^t x) = f(x) + t" ⟺ parallelizable is a one-line restatement of Theorem 1 and is correct on any metric space. But the words "time function" are the sweep's, not the paper's, and the paper never writes the additivity identity. The adjudication's own caveat (line 62: "O's gloss … not a quotation") is confirmed. Note also the direction that matters for C3: **continuity** of the function is the whole content (Def. 1 gives a section, hence some t_s, for every flow without closed orbits and rest points; Theorem 1 is that parallelizable ⟺ t_s can be taken continuous). The program's W is only l.s.c., so on the finite locus log W is an additive function that is **not** of the kind Theorem 1 requires; the adjudication's phrase "a positive function conjugating the flow to translation" overstates what an l.s.c. additive function does (it does not give a homeomorphism to S × ℝ). Suggested corrected wording: "a **continuous** real function additive along orbits, f(φ^t x) = f(x) + t, is equivalent to a section with continuous return time and hence to parallelizability (Dugundji–Antosiewicz 1961, Definition 1, Theorem 1, p. 544, any metric space); the program's log W is additive but only lower semicontinuous, so it is a parallelizing datum only in the weakened sense that it still forbids compact invariant sets."

2. *"parallelizable ⟺ dispersive"* — **accurate**, with the hypothesis the paper states: locally compact separable metric space (Theorem 3, p. 548); parallelizable ⟹ dispersive holds on any metric space (p. 545, first sentence).

3. *"⟺ all limit sets empty ⟺ no nonempty compact invariant set"* — **not in Dugundji–Antosiewicz.** These are Akin–Auslander Thm 6.3(b)(iv) "Ωφ = ∅" (and the Bhatia–Szegő / Nemytskii–Stepanov chain), not this paper. The paper's third equivalent is "unstable flow with no improper saddle point" (p. 548). Note that "dispersive" in Dugundji–Antosiewicz (Montgomery–Zippin's definition, p. 545, a pairwise condition on neighborhoods) is a stronger-looking condition than "all limit sets empty" (Nemytskii–Stepanov's "unstable", their footnote 3 says only that semi-orbits are not contained in compact sets); the equivalence of the two on locally compact separable spaces is exactly the paper's content plus the sentence after Theorem 3. So the sweep's chain is correct on such spaces but should be attributed to Akin–Auslander Thm 6.3 / Bhatia–Szegő for the "limit set" and "compact invariant set" clauses and to Dugundji–Antosiewicz Thm 3 only for parallelizable ⟺ dispersive.

4. *"the classical parallelizable/dispersive framework … assumes locally compact state spaces and a globally wandering flow"* (qstar §4 NOVELTY block) — **accurate** for Theorems 2–3 (locally compact separable metric; every point wandering = completely unstable, p. 546); Definition 2 and Theorem 1, however, are stated on an **arbitrary metric space** (p. 543 "arbitrary metric space R"; Introduction: "Part I deals with flows on arbitrary metric spaces and answers completely the question when they are parallelizable"). The record should not say the section/continuous-t_s characterization needs local compactness; only the dispersive ⟹ parallelizable direction does.

5. *"Thm 6.1 (a Lyapunov function L on a φ-open U with L(x) = 0, L(f(x)) = 1 characterizes wandering points)"* (qstar REPAIR block) — that is Akin–Auslander's formulation. The Dugundji–Antosiewicz original is Lemma 3 (p. 545): a wandering point of a flow on a locally compact space has an open ∞-tube U = f(S, E) on which the flow is parallelizable (t_s continuous on U). Citation should say "Lemma 3, p. 545" if the 1961 paper is named for it.

**Plain-English reading.** The 1961 paper says: a flow is "parallelizable" (looks globally like straight lines S × ℝ) exactly when it has a cross-section S hit once by every orbit with the crossing time varying continuously (Theorem 1, any metric space), and on locally compact separable spaces that is the same as "dispersive" (every pair of points has neighborhoods that stop interacting after a bounded time, Theorem 3). The program's sentence compresses this correctly except that (i) the label "time function" and the additivity formula are the program's paraphrase, (ii) the "limit sets empty / no compact invariant set" links come from later authors, and (iii) the author order is Dugundji–Antosiewicz. Nothing here changes any verdict; C3/C4 stay PARTIAL on the same anchor.

### Item 16 — Sullivan 1976 and Plante 1975 (quotations verified by vision on printed pp. 231, 237, 238, 240 of Sullivan and pp. 330, 335, 339, 348 of Plante; the rest from the text layer, prose only)

#### Sullivan, Invent. Math. 36 (1976)

**Standing hypotheses for Part II (p. 238, §4):** "For this discussion it is enough to assume that the ambient manifold M is of class C^∞, and foliation is of class C¹. (Many of our statements are true under weaker hypothesis.)" Footnote 3 (p. 236): "This is the point where the C¹-hypotheses is used. Otherwise the computation works for locally rectifiable leaves with continuously varying plane field". M is a **manifold** throughout; "compact" is assumed where stated.

**Structure cycles (p. 230, Definition I.6):** "If C is a cone structure, the structure cycles of C are the structure currents which are closed as currents." (Structure currents = currents in the closed cone generated by Dirac currents of the cone field, Definition I.4/Prop. I.5; for an oriented foliation these are the currents positive on the foliation's tangent p-vectors.)

**The Hahn–Banach dichotomy (p. 231, Theorem I.7):** "Suppose C is a cone structure of p-vectors defined on a compact subspace X in the interior of M which is also compact (with or without boundary). **Theorem I.7.** i) *There are always non-trivial structure cycles in X or closed p-forms on M transversal to the cone structure (i.e. positive on the cones C_x).* ii) *If no closed transverse form exists some non-trivial structure cycle in X is homologous to zero in M (type I).* iii) *If no non-trivial structure cycle exists some transversal closed form is cohomologous to zero (Type III).* iv) *If there are both structures cycles and transversal closed forms (type II) then a) the natural map (structure cycles on X → homology classes in M) is proper and the image is a compact cone ℂ ⊂ H_p(M, R) b) the interior of the dual cone ℂ′ ⊂ H^p(M, R) consists precisely of the classes of closed forms transverse to C.*" The proof (p. 231) is four sentences of Hahn–Banach: "First we note that the closed currents 𝒵 forms a closed subspace of the p-currents on M (∂ is continuous). Second the boundaries form a closed subspace of the cycles 𝒵 … Third by the Hahn Banach theorem any closed subspace of currents which does not intersect a compact set can be extended to a closed subspace of codimension one not intersecting that set. Fourth, a closed hyperplane of currents determines (up to a non-zero constant) a C^∞ form on M which vanishes only on that hyperplane because currents and forms are dual spaces [Sc]."

**Local structure of foliation cycles (p. 235, Theorem I.12):** "We assume the foliation is of class C¹ and choose a closed neighborhood B foliated by closed disks L_y on leaves. Let D denote the quotient of B by the leaves L_y, D = {y}. Let (L_y) denote the current of integration on L_y (oriented by ℱ). Suppose Z is any foliation cycle whose support intersects B. **Theorem I.12.** *There is a non-negative measure µ on D so that on interior B the current Z may be represented ∫_D (L_y) dµ. µ is unique in the interior of D.*"

**Definition of transversal invariant measure (p. 236–237):** "Denote by T a finite union of closed disks transverse to the foliation whose interiors meet every leaf. If a path on one leaf connects two points x and y in T with y in the interior the foliation determines a homeomorphism germ, embedding a neighborhood of x in D into one of y in D. The foliation is essentially determined by this data. **Definition.** A *transversal invariant measure* for the foliation is a non-negative measure of finite mass on T which is compatible with all the germs of homeomorphisms determined by the foliation. *Remark.* i) Any other transversal disk inherits a measure by sliding it piecemeal into T. … ii) This intrinsic transversal measure of a foliation of dimension p together with an orientation determine a closed p-current [RS]. One merely integrates local forms over the leaves and averages the result in the transverse direction with the measure. The invariance property of the measure makes the procedure independant of the way a global form is broken into local forms by a partition of unity [RS], [EMS]. The current just described is clearly a foliation cycle."

**The correspondence (p. 237, Theorem I.13):** "**Theorem I.13.** *If ℱ is an oriented p-dimensional foliation of a compact manifold, invariant transversal measures and foliation cycles are in a canonical one to one correspondance.* Proof. Theorem I.12 tells us how to construct an invariant measure from a foliation cycle Z. Cover the manifold by flow boxes (whose interiors cover) B₁, B₂, …, B_n. Express Z locally in B_i as ∫_{D_i} (L_y) dµ_i. The µ_i determine the measure on the transversal T = ∪ D^i. The invariance of {µ_i} can be reduced to the question of compatibility for the case of one small flow box contained inside a larger one. This case is clear from the statement of I.12. We have described above (and in [RS]) the procedure in the other direction. The two compositions are each the identity."

**The "no foliation cycles ⟺ …" results (p. 238, Theorems II.1, II.2):** "In §3 we have shown how foliation cycles correspond to transversal invariant measures and we refer to the two notions interchangeably (fixing the orientation of ℱ (Theorem I.13). Then we have **Theorem II.1.** *Suppose M is compact and ℱ is an orientable p-dimensional foliation of M. Then one either has i) a non-trivial foliation cycle (equivalently a non-trivial transversal invariant measure) or ii) a closed p-form transverse to the foliation (namely, a closed p-form positive on the foliation).* In fact we have the more precise statement, **Theorem II.2.** i) *If there is no transversal invariant measure for ℱ there is an exact p-form positive on the leaves of ℱ.* ii) *If there is no closed form positive on the leaves of ℱ then some transversal invariant measure determines a foliation cycle in the trivial homology class.*" (p. 239) "Theorems II.1, II.2, II.3, II.4 are reinterpretations of (I.7) in the context of foliations possible because of (I.13). They are also true in the relative case. Namely if K is a compact subset of M one considers foliation cycles with support in K and closed forms positive on the tangent directions of ℱ along K. Then the theorems are true on and near K (Theorem I.7)." Example (p. 238): "The Anosov foliations of the unit tangent bundle of a negative curved surface do not have transverse measures. (Note Theorem II.2 is clear in this case. One pulls up the volume form on the base to get the positive form. This form is exact because the Euler characteristic is non-zero.)"

**Geometry of leaves — Sullivan's generalization of Plante (pp. 239–240):** "Here we generalize the theorem of Plante [P] that a leaf of subexponential growth determines a transversal measure. **Definition II.5.** A non-compact complete Riemannian manifold L is "not closed at ∞" if it is possible to solve the equation dη = ω where ω is a bounded volume form … and η is a bounded form (see [SW]). Otherwise we say L is "closed at ∞"." "**Corollary II.7.** [SW]. *If L is "not closed at ∞" then L has exponential growth of volume.*" "**Theorem II.8.** *If M is compact and ℱ does not have an invariant transversal measure then every leaf is "not closed at ∞" (and satisfies the uniform exponential growth described above). Furthermore, if some leaf L is "closed at ∞" there must be an invariant transversal measure whose support is contained in the closure of L.* Proof. The first part follows immediately from II.2 and the definition. The second part is the relative form." "Conclusion. … For in fact Plante considers a sequence of regions R_i on leaves so that the ratio area ∂R_i/volume R_i → 0 to define homology classes. Such a "Plante sequence" may be viewed in our terms as determining a specific sequence of foliation currents C_i = (1/volume R_i)·(∫_{R_i}(·)) which i) have mass 1 and thus form a precompact family of currents {C_i}, ii) can only accumulate to foliation cycles because the (volume-area) condition insures mass (∂C_i) → 0. The accumulation points of a "Plante sequence" determine non-trivial foliation cycles and thus non trivial transversal invariant measures."

**Recurrence set and stability (p. 240):** "**Definition II.9.** The union of the supports of all foliation cycles (or invariant transverse measures) of a foliation ℱ is called the *Poincaré recurrence set of ℱ*, and denoted P(ℱ). … For general foliations P(ℱ) contains all compact leaves and intersects the closure of those non-compact leaves which are "closed at ∞" (II.8). **Proposition II.10.** *P(ℱ) is a closed invariant set when M is compact.* … **Theorem II.11.** *Suppose ℱ has no invariant transversal measure. #### Plante, Ann. of Math. 102 (1975)

**Definition of the holonomy pseudogroup and of an invariant measure (pp. 329–330, §1):** "Let M be a C^∞ paracompact manifold and let ℱ be a foliation of M of codimension k ≥ 1. We assume that ℱ is transversely oriented and of class C^r (r ≥ 0) in the sense of [2]. If P and Q are two k-dimensional submanifolds (possibly with boundary) which are everywhere transverse to ℱ, then ℱ gives rise to a collection (possibly empty) of C^r homeomorphisms between subsets of P and Q which are determined by restricting deformations on M which preserve each leaf of ℱ. The collection of all such homeomorphisms between subsets of all possible pairs of transverse manifolds generates the holonomy pseudogroup of ℱ." (p. 329, after the five pseudogroup axioms) "It should be noted that we do not make the customary assumption that the domains and ranges of elements of Γ are open sets. … Let Γ be a pseudogroup of homeomorphisms of a topological space X and let µ be a non-negative, finitely additive measure defined on the ring of subsets of X generated by the compact sets. It will always be assumed that X is Hausdorff and that the µ-measure of any compact set is finite. We say (p. 330) that µ is Γ-*invariant* if for every γ ∈ Γ and measurable set A ⊂ 𝒟(γ) we have µ(γ(A)) = µ(A). **Definition.** A foliation ℱ is said to have *measure preserving holonomy* if its holonomy pseudogroup has a non-trivial invariant measure which is finite on compact sets. Such a measure is called *holonomy invariant* or ℱ-*invariant*. If µ is an ℱ-invariant measure its *support* (denoted supp µ) is the set of points x ∈ M with the following property: If N is a k-dimensional submanifold transverse to ℱ which contains x as an interior point then µ(N) > 0. The support of an ℱ-invariant measure is a closed set which is ℱ-saturated, i.e., it is a union of leaves of ℱ. *Remark.* It will turn out that the measures constructed in later sections are actually σ-additive. However, the finite additivity we have assumed will be adequate …" Examples (p. 330): "(1) If the foliation ℱ is determined by a closed k-form Ω then the restriction of Ω to a transverse k-manifold determines a volume element which gives a measure that is positive on open sets. Since Ω is closed this measure is ℱ-invariant. (2) If L is a closed leaf of ℱ then we may define a measure on any transverse k-manifold N as follows. For any set A ⊂ N let µ(A) be the number of elements in the set A ∩ L. Clearly µ is ℱ-invariant, and if A is compact then µ(A) is finite."

**The cohomology class of an invariant measure — the Ruelle–Sullivan current (pp. 330–331):** "When ℱ is transversely oriented an invariant measure determines a cohomology class. There are different ways to define this class. The definition we give here is in [20] and is implicit in [4]. … Let {U_i} be a locally finite covering of M by distinguished neighborhoods … {λ_i} be a smooth partition of unity subordinate to {U_i}, ψ_i: D^k × D^{m−k} → U_i distinguished coordinate maps, and η a closed (m − k)-form on M with compact support. Define Φ_µ(η) = Σ_i ± ∫_{D^k} [∫_{{x}×D^{m−k}} ψ_i^*(λ_i η)] d(ψ_i^*µ)(x) where the + or − is chosen according to whether the orientation on D_i induced from ℱ via ψ_i is the same or opposite the standard orientation. ψ_i^*µ denotes the measure induced on D^k from µ via ψ_i, and this measure is independent of the point in D^{m−k} by ℱ-invariance of µ. It is easily seen that Φ_µ defines an element in the dual space of H^{m−k}(M; R) since if η = dθ then η = Σ_i d(λ_i θ) and Φ_µ(d(λ_i θ)) = 0 by Stokes' Theorem. We think of Φ_µ as being an element of H^k(M; R). It can be shown that Φ_µ is independent of the covering and the partition of unity chosen." ([20] = Ruelle–Sullivan; [4] = Hirsch–Thurston.) "**Theorem 1.1.** *Let M be a C^∞ manifold and ℱ a transversely oriented codimension k foliation of M of class C^r (r ≥ 0 if k = 1, r ≥ 1 otherwise). If N is a compact k-dimensional submanifold without boundary transverse to ℱ which intersects the support of an ℱ-invariant measure, then N represents a non-zero element of H_k(M; R).*" (p. 331; "may be thought of as an abstract Poincaré–Bendixson theorem for foliations").

**Existence via growth — the pseudogroup theorem (pp. 334–335, Theorem 3.1):** growth function "g_x(n) = |Γⁿ(x)| … If lim inf_{n→∞} (1/n) log g_x(n) > 0 Γ is said to have *exponential growth* at x. Otherwise, Γ has *non-exponential growth* at x." "**Theorem 3.1.** *Let Γ be a countably generated pseudogroup of homeomorphisms of a compact metric space X. If Γ has non-exponential growth at x ∈ X then there exists a Borel measure µ on X with the following properties. (i) µ is Γ-invariant. (ii) µ(X) = 1. (iii) supp µ ⊂ closure of Γ(x).*" The proof (p. 335) is the averaging argument: "Let n_j → ∞ be a sequence of positive integers such that lim_{j→∞} |Γ^{n_j+1}(x) − Γ^{n_j−1}(x)| / |Γ^{n_j}(x)| = 0. That this can be done follows from the assumption that Γ has non-exponential growth at x. Now for each j, define the normalized functional I_j by I_j(f) = (1/|Γ^{n_j}(x)|) Σ_{y∈Γ^{n_j}(x)} f(y). Taking a subsequence, if necessary, we may assume that the functionals I_j converge in the weak topology to a normalized functional I. We claim that I is Γ-invariant." **The word "Følner" does not occur in either paper** (text-layer search); Plante's sequence (Γ^{n_j}(x)) with vanishing relative boundary is the Følner condition without the name, and Sullivan calls the leafwise version a "Plante sequence" (p. 240).

**Existence via growth — the foliation theorem (p. 339, Theorem 4.1, verified by vision):** "The following gives a sufficient condition for the existence of a holonomy invariant measure when M is compact. We will see in Section 6 that it is also a necessary condition for codimension one foliations. **Theorem 4.1.** *Let ℱ be a codimension k foliation of a compact manifold M. If L is a leaf of ℱ having non-exponential growth then there exists a non-trivial holonomy invariant measure for ℱ which is finite on compact sets and which has support contained in the closure of L.*" (Growth of a leaf is defined on pp. 337–339 by plaque counting for a regular covering, shown equivalent to Riemannian volume growth for smooth foliations, p. 339.) "**Corollary 4.2.** *Let ℱ be a transversely oriented codimension k foliation of a compact manifold M of class C^r (r ≥ 0 if k = 1, r ≥ 1 otherwise), N a compact k-dimensional submanifold (without boundary) transverse to ℱ, and L a leaf of ℱ having non-exponential growth. If every leaf in the closure of L intersects N, then N represents a non-zero element in H_k(M; R).*" (p. 340)

**Codimension one — the converse (p. 348, verified by vision):** "**Corollary 6.4.** *Let M be a compact manifold and ℱ a transversely oriented codimension one foliation of M of class C^r (r ≥ 0). Then either (i) every leaf of ℱ has exponential growth, or (ii) some leaf of ℱ has polynomial growth of degree max{0, b₁(M) − 1}. In particular, if rank H₁(M; R) ≤ 1 then (ii) says that ℱ has a compact leaf.* **Corollary 6.5.** *For a codimension one foliation ℱ of a compact manifold M the following are equivalent. (i) ℱ has a leaf with non-exponential growth. (ii) ℱ has a leaf with polynomial growth. (iii) ℱ has a non-trivial holonomy invariant measure.*" Also Theorem 6.3 (p. 346): a leaf in the support of an invariant measure has polynomial growth of degree max{0, p(µ) − 1}, p(µ) the rank of the period group of Φ_µ. Introduction (p. 328): "In general, the existence of leaves with non-exponential growth implies the existence of a holonomy invariant measure. There is no general converse to this statement. However, for codimension one foliations of compact manifolds, the existence of an invariant measure implies the existence of leaves having polynomial growth …"

**Plante on Ruelle–Sullivan (p. 359, §10):** "We have made extensive use of the fact that an invariant measure for a transversely oriented foliation determines a cohomology class. … the foliation only needs to be defined for the support of the measure. This is the basis for the definition of a "geometric current" defined for a "partial foliation" given by Ruelle–Sullivan [20]."

**Plain-English reading.** Both papers say what the program has been assuming: a holonomy-invariant transverse measure is the same thing as a closed positive current tangent to the leaves (Sullivan I.13), such a current pairs to zero with exact forms (Plante p. 331), and a foliation of a compact manifold either has such a measure or has an exact form positive along the leaves (Sullivan II.1/II.2) — with the existence supplied by a leaf of subexponential growth (Plante 4.1; Sullivan II.8 more generally). ## §4 Other things in these sources a future session should know exist

- **Sullivan Theorem II.26, p. 247** (cross-relevant to **item 13 / C3**, not to item 16): for a non-singular flow and a compact set K in a manifold, "exactly one of the following: i) K contains a closed invariant set; ii) There is a gradient-like function f defined near K (that is, df is positive on the flow directions)." This is the printed form of "a function strictly increasing along orbits near K ⟺ no compact invariant set in K" — closer to the C3 device (a positive function increasing along the flow forbids compact invariant sets) than anything in Dugundji–Antosiewicz, which is about global sections. It is a Lyapunov-type (monotone) function, not an additive one, on a manifold. Sullivan Theorem III.1 (pp. 248–249) is the Lorentz analogue and names Hawking's theorem: "A (non-compact) space-time has a cosmic time function iff it is stably causal" — the only place in these four files where the words "time function" occur.
- **Sullivan Theorem II.27 (Schwartzman), p. 247**: every invariant measure of a flow non-trivial in H₁ ⟹ C^∞ global cross section (Abel–Tischler argument). The flow-with-section theme of item 13 in homological form.
- **Sullivan Theorem II.8, p. 240**: no invariant transversal measure ⟹ every leaf "not closed at ∞" with uniform exponential growth (isoperimetric inequality vol R ≤ γ area ∂R, Prop. II.6). Bears on the s16 N-B discussion (hyperbolic preserved leaves): a compact lamination with no invariant measure has all leaves of uniform exponential growth — but Sullivan proves it for manifolds only.
- **Sullivan Theorem II.11, p. 240**: absence of invariant transversal measures is stable under C⁰-perturbation of the tangent planes.
- **Sullivan p. 238, Example ii)**: Anosov foliations of the unit tangent bundle of a negatively curved surface have no transverse measures (exact positive form from the base volume form).
- **Sullivan Proposition II.24, p. 246**: for flows, flow-invariant measures ↔ foliation cycles along the flow.
- **Sullivan Theorem I.11, p. 235**: intersection numbers of transverse structure cycles are positive; used for Plante's Theorem 1.1 (Sullivan cites "Theorem 1.1 [P], or Proposition 2 [RS]" on p. 242).
- **Plante Theorem 6.3, p. 346 / Theorem 6.6, p. 348**: a leaf in the support of an invariant measure of a transversely oriented codimension-one foliation has polynomial growth of degree max{0, p(µ) − 1}; on non-compact M such a leaf is closed or p(µ) ≥ 2. Here p(µ) is the rank of the period group of the cohomology class Φ_µ — a **period-group** statement in the same vocabulary as the program's length-group discussions (y0 §7).
- **Plante §2, pp. 332–334**: foliated bundles; Prop. 2.2 amenable structure group ⟹ invariant measure; "In particular, this is the case whenever π₁(B) is amenable (e.g., solvable)" (p. 334).
- **Plante §9, pp. 357–359**: locally free actions of amenable unimodular Lie groups of codimension one carry invariant measures (Cor. 9.3–9.5); Prop. 9.6 no compact exceptional minimal sets for C² locally free amenable unimodular actions of codimension one.
- **Plante Lemma 3.2, p. 336**: extension of an invariant measure from a compact transversal K to all transversals, unique.
- **Dugundji–Antosiewicz Theorem 4, p. 550**: with no stability hypothesis, a nonautonomous ODE flow on Eⁿ × E admits a continuous V, 0 ≤ V ≤ 1, non-increasing along orbits, positive definite on a nested family of tubes; Theorem 6 (p. 552) gives ∂V/∂t ≤ −V under uniform asymptotic stability; §6 (pp. 554–555) smoothness of sections (Whitney extension).
